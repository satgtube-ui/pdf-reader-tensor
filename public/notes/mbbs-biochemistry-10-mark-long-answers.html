1Introduction to Biochemistry

Top
OpeningCorePathwayClinicalFinish
1 mark4 marks2 marks2 marks1 mark

Definition and importance

Biochemistry is the study of the chemical constituents of living cells and the reactions by which they obtain energy, grow, communicate and maintain homeostasis.

Core answer

  • Major domains are structural biochemistry, enzymology, intermediary metabolism, molecular biology, nutrition and clinical biochemistry.
  • The cell is an open system: nutrients and oxygen enter, useful work is performed, and carbon dioxide, urea, water and heat leave.
  • Catabolism breaks complex molecules and generates ATP, NADH and FADH2. Anabolism consumes ATP and reducing power, mainly NADPH, to synthesize cellular components.
  • Metabolic pathways are compartmentalized. Glycolysis occurs in cytosol; TCA cycle and beta oxidation mainly in mitochondria; fatty-acid synthesis in cytosol; protein processing in ER and Golgi.
  • Homeostasis is maintained by enzymes, hormones, transporters, feedback control and gene expression. Insulin favors storage; glucagon and catecholamines favor mobilization.
  • Biochemical measurements connect mechanism with disease: glucose in diabetes, bilirubin in jaundice, creatinine in renal failure, enzymes in tissue injury and DNA tests in inherited disorders.

Flowchart or diagram to draw

Genes -> proteins and enzymes -> metabolism and signaling -> cell function -> health or disease

Clinical relevance

  • Disease may result from enzyme deficiency, abnormal protein structure, nutritional deficiency, toxin exposure or dysregulated signaling.
  • A good clinical interpretation combines the analyte, specimen, reference interval, timing, organ function and pre-analytical factors.

Exam finish Conclude that biochemistry explains normal function at the molecular level and provides the basis for diagnosis, prevention and rational treatment.

2Cells and Cell Organelles

Top
OpeningCorePathwayClinicalFinish
1 mark4 marks2 marks2 marks1 mark

Definition and importance

The cell is the structural and functional unit of life. Eukaryotic cells contain membrane-bound organelles that create specialized biochemical compartments.

Core answer

  • Plasma membrane is a fluid phospholipid bilayer containing cholesterol, proteins and carbohydrates. It mediates transport, receptors, adhesion and signal transduction.
  • Nucleus stores DNA; the nucleolus synthesizes rRNA and assembles ribosomal subunits. Nuclear pores regulate nucleocytoplasmic traffic.
  • Mitochondria contain outer and inner membranes, matrix enzymes and mitochondrial DNA. They perform TCA cycle, beta oxidation, oxidative phosphorylation and apoptosis signaling.
  • Rough ER synthesizes secreted, lysosomal and membrane proteins. Smooth ER synthesizes lipids and steroids, detoxifies drugs and stores calcium.
  • Golgi apparatus modifies glycoproteins, sorts cargo and forms secretory vesicles and lysosomes. Mannose-6-phosphate targets enzymes to lysosomes.
  • Lysosomes contain acid hydrolases for intracellular digestion; peroxisomes oxidize very-long-chain fatty acids and detoxify hydrogen peroxide by catalase.
  • Ribosomes translate mRNA. Cytoskeleton includes microfilaments, intermediate filaments and microtubules for shape, motility, transport and mitosis.
  • Transport across membrane occurs by simple diffusion, channels, facilitated diffusion, primary active transport, secondary active transport, endocytosis and exocytosis.

Flowchart or diagram to draw

Plasma membrane | Cytosol and cytoskeleton | ER-Golgi-lysosome system | Nucleus | Mitochondria and peroxisomes

Clinical relevance

  • Lysosomal enzyme defects cause storage diseases; peroxisomal defects cause Zellweger spectrum disorders; mitochondrial mutations affect high-energy tissues.
  • Failure of LDL-receptor endocytosis causes familial hypercholesterolemia, illustrating the clinical importance of membrane trafficking.

3Carbohydrate Chemistry 1

Top
OpeningCorePathwayClinicalFinish
1 mark4 marks2 marks2 marks1 mark

Definition and importance

Carbohydrates are polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones, or substances that yield them on hydrolysis. They provide energy, storage, structure and recognition signals.

Core answer

  • Monosaccharides are classified by carbon number and functional group: glucose is an aldohexose, fructose a ketohexose and ribose an aldopentose.
  • Stereoisomers have the same formula but different configuration. D and L forms are based on glyceraldehyde; most human sugars are D sugars.
  • Epimers differ at one asymmetric carbon: glucose and galactose at C4; glucose and mannose at C2. Aldose-ketose pairs such as glucose and fructose are functional isomers.
  • Cyclization forms hemiacetals or hemiketals. Alpha and beta anomers differ at the anomeric carbon and interconvert by mutarotation.
  • Reducing sugars have a free anomeric carbon and reduce copper or silver reagents. All monosaccharides, lactose and maltose are reducing; sucrose is non-reducing.
  • Important reactions are oxidation to acids, reduction to sugar alcohols, ester formation such as glucose-6-phosphate, glycoside formation and osazone formation.

Flowchart or diagram to draw

Linear glucose <-> cyclic alpha-D-glucose and beta-D-glucose by mutarotation

Clinical relevance

  • Sorbitol accumulation contributes to cataract, neuropathy and retinopathy in diabetes; galactitol contributes to cataract in galactosemia.
  • Glucose oxidase methods are more specific than older nonspecific reducing tests for estimating blood or urine glucose.

4Carbohydrate Chemistry 2

Top
OpeningCorePathwayClinicalFinish
1 mark4 marks2 marks2 marks1 mark

Definition and importance

Oligosaccharides and polysaccharides are formed by glycosidic linkage of monosaccharides and serve dietary, storage, structural and recognition functions.

Core answer

  • Maltose is glucose-alpha(1->4)-glucose; lactose is galactose-beta(1->4)-glucose; sucrose is glucose-alpha1<->beta2-fructose.
  • Starch contains amylose with alpha(1->4) links and branched amylopectin with alpha(1->6) branch points. Glycogen is more highly branched and rapidly mobilized.
  • Cellulose contains beta(1->4)-linked glucose. Humans lack cellulase, so cellulose acts as dietary fiber.
  • Glycosaminoglycans are repeating disaccharides, usually acidic and sulfated. Examples include hyaluronic acid, chondroitin sulfate, heparin, dermatan sulfate and keratan sulfate.
  • Proteoglycans contain a core protein with long GAG chains and organize extracellular matrix; glycoproteins have shorter branched oligosaccharides and function in receptors, antibodies and cell recognition.
  • Blood-group substances and many membrane receptors depend on precise oligosaccharide sequences. Glycation is nonenzymatic attachment of sugar to proteins; glycosylation is enzyme-directed.

Flowchart or diagram to draw

Monosaccharides -> disaccharides and oligosaccharides -> storage polysaccharides, structural polysaccharides and glycoconjugates

Clinical relevance

  • Lactase deficiency causes osmotic diarrhea and fermentation after milk intake. Mucopolysaccharidoses result from lysosomal GAG degradation defects.
  • HbA1c reflects nonenzymatic glycation and approximates average glycemia over the previous 2-3 months.

5Extracellular Matrix

Top
OpeningCorePathwayClinicalFinish
1 mark4 marks2 marks2 marks1 mark

Definition and importance

Extracellular matrix is the organized network of proteins and polysaccharides outside cells that provides mechanical support and regulates adhesion, migration, growth and differentiation.

Core answer

  • Collagen is the major fibrous protein. Its basic unit is tropocollagen containing three alpha chains in a right-handed triple helix with repeating Gly-X-Y sequences.
  • Collagen synthesis includes hydroxylation of proline and lysine requiring vitamin C, glycosylation, triple-helix formation, secretion, extracellular cleavage and lysyl-oxidase cross-linking requiring copper.
  • Type I occurs in bone, skin and tendon; type II in cartilage; type III in reticulin and vessels; type IV forms basement-membrane networks.
  • Elastin provides recoil in lung, arteries and ligaments. Desmosine cross-links are formed by lysyl oxidase; fibrillin forms the microfibrillar scaffold.
  • Fibronectin and laminin are adhesive glycoproteins. Integrins connect ECM to cytoskeleton and transmit bidirectional signals.
  • Proteoglycans retain water, resist compression and bind growth factors. Matrix metalloproteinases remodel ECM and are controlled by tissue inhibitors.

Flowchart or diagram to draw

Cell surface integrin <-> fibronectin or laminin <-> collagen, elastin and hydrated proteoglycan gel

Clinical relevance

  • Vitamin C deficiency causes scurvy; defective type I collagen causes osteogenesis imperfecta; type IV collagen defects cause Alport syndrome.
  • Fibrillin-1 defects cause Marfan syndrome; excessive collagen deposition produces fibrosis; tumor invasion requires altered adhesion and matrix degradation.

6Lipid Chemistry 1

Top
OpeningCorePathwayClinicalFinish
1 mark4 marks2 marks2 marks1 mark

Definition and importance

Lipids are heterogeneous water-insoluble biomolecules soluble in nonpolar solvents. They store energy, form membranes and act as signaling molecules.

Core answer

  • Simple lipids are triacylglycerols and waxes; compound lipids include phospholipids, glycolipids and lipoproteins; derived lipids include fatty acids, steroids and fat-soluble vitamins.
  • Fatty acids may be saturated or unsaturated. Cis double bonds introduce kinks and increase fluidity; longer chain length and saturation increase melting point.
  • Essential fatty acids are linoleic acid and alpha-linolenic acid; arachidonic acid becomes conditionally essential when linoleate supply or conversion is inadequate.
  • Triacylglycerol is the major energy store. Hydrolysis yields glycerol and fatty acids; saponification forms soap; oxidative rancidity damages unsaturated fats.
  • Phosphatidylcholine, phosphatidylethanolamine, phosphatidylserine, phosphatidylinositol and cardiolipin are major glycerophospholipids.
  • Sphingomyelin contains ceramide plus phosphocholine. Cerebrosides, globosides and gangliosides are glycosphingolipids important in nervous tissue and cell recognition.

Flowchart or diagram to draw

Fatty acids + glycerol -> triacylglycerol; fatty acids + glycerol or sphingosine + polar head -> membrane lipid

Clinical relevance

  • Essential-fatty-acid deficiency causes scaly dermatitis, alopecia, poor growth and impaired wound healing.
  • Sphingolipid degradation defects cause lysosomal storage disorders such as Tay-Sachs, Gaucher, Niemann-Pick and metachromatic leukodystrophy.

7Lipid Chemistry 2

Top
OpeningCorePathwayClinicalFinish
1 mark4 marks2 marks2 marks1 mark

Definition and importance

Complex and derived lipids control membrane architecture, transport and signaling; cholesterol is the central sterol in human tissues.

Core answer

  • Cholesterol has four fused rings, a hydroxyl group, hydrocarbon tail and double bond. It is amphipathic and buffers membrane fluidity.
  • Cholesterol is the precursor of bile acids, steroid hormones and vitamin D. Cholesteryl esters are the storage and transport form.
  • Phospholipases A1, A2, C and D cleave specific bonds. Phospholipase A2 releases arachidonic acid for eicosanoid synthesis.
  • Eicosanoids include prostaglandins, prostacyclin, thromboxanes and leukotrienes. Cyclooxygenase forms prostanoids; lipoxygenase forms leukotrienes and lipoxins.
  • Lipid bilayers are fluid mosaics with asymmetric lipid distribution. Lipid rafts enriched in cholesterol and sphingolipids organize signaling proteins.
  • Lipoproteins package hydrophobic lipids with apoproteins. Their density rises as the protein-to-lipid ratio increases.

Flowchart or diagram to draw

Membrane phospholipid --PLA2--> arachidonic acid --COX/LOX--> prostanoids or leukotrienes

Clinical relevance

  • NSAIDs inhibit cyclooxygenase; glucocorticoids reduce eicosanoid synthesis partly by inhibiting phospholipase A2 signaling and COX-2 expression.
  • Surfactant dipalmitoyl phosphatidylcholine lowers alveolar surface tension; deficiency contributes to neonatal respiratory distress syndrome.

8Protein Chemistry 1

Top
OpeningCorePathwayClinicalFinish
1 mark4 marks2 marks2 marks1 mark

Definition and importance

Proteins are polymers of alpha-amino acids linked by peptide bonds and folded into specific three-dimensional structures.

Core answer

  • Amino acids contain an alpha carbon attached to amino, carboxyl, hydrogen and side-chain groups, except glycine which is achiral.
  • They are classified as nonpolar, polar uncharged, acidic, basic, aromatic or sulfur-containing. Nutritionally they are essential, nonessential or conditionally essential.
  • Amino acids are ampholytes and exist mainly as zwitterions at physiological pH. The isoelectric pH is the pH at which net charge is zero.
  • Peptide bonds are planar and have partial double-bond character. Protein direction is written from N-terminus to C-terminus.
  • Primary structure is the amino-acid sequence. Secondary structure includes alpha helix, beta sheet and beta turns stabilized mainly by hydrogen bonds.
  • Tertiary structure is the three-dimensional fold stabilized by hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds, ionic interactions, van der Waals forces and disulfide bonds.
  • Quaternary structure is association of subunits, as in hemoglobin. Motifs and domains are recurring structural or functional units.

Flowchart or diagram to draw

Amino-acid sequence -> local secondary structure -> folded domain -> multisubunit protein

Clinical relevance

  • A single amino-acid substitution can cause disease, exemplified by beta6 Glu-to-Val in sickle hemoglobin.
  • Protein misfolding and aggregation occur in amyloidosis and several neurodegenerative diseases.

9Protein Chemistry 2

Top
OpeningCorePathwayClinicalFinish
1 mark4 marks2 marks2 marks1 mark

Definition and importance

Protein properties arise from amino-acid composition, conformation, solubility and interactions with water, salts, acids and other molecules.

Core answer

  • Proteins may be simple or conjugated. Conjugated proteins include glycoproteins, lipoproteins, metalloproteins, phosphoproteins, chromoproteins and nucleoproteins.
  • Globular proteins are compact and functional in catalysis, transport or defense; fibrous proteins such as collagen and keratin provide structure.
  • Denaturation disrupts secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure without usually breaking peptide bonds. Causes include heat, extremes of pH, organic solvents, detergents and heavy metals.
  • Denaturation causes loss of biological activity, reduced solubility and altered viscosity. Some proteins can renature if primary structure remains intact.
  • Salting in at low salt concentration increases solubility; salting out at high concentration precipitates proteins. Ammonium sulfate fractionation uses this principle.
  • Protein separation methods include electrophoresis, chromatography, ultracentrifugation and dialysis. Detection uses Biuret, Lowry, Bradford or immunochemical methods.
  • Chaperones assist folding and limit aggregation; ubiquitin targets many damaged or short-lived proteins to the proteasome.

Flowchart or diagram to draw

Native protein --stress--> unfolded or misfolded protein --chaperone/refolding or ubiquitin-proteasome degradation

Clinical relevance

  • Heat coagulates albumin; heavy-metal binding causes protein precipitation and toxicity. Monoclonal immunoglobulin may produce an M band on electrophoresis.
  • Prion disease illustrates a self-propagating abnormal protein conformation without nucleic-acid transmission.

10Enzymes 1

Top
OpeningCorePathwayClinicalFinish
1 mark4 marks2 marks2 marks1 mark

Definition and importance

Enzymes are biological catalysts that accelerate reactions by lowering activation energy without changing reaction equilibrium or being consumed.

Core answer

  • Most enzymes are proteins; a few RNA molecules are ribozymes. The protein part is apoenzyme, nonprotein component is cofactor, and complete active enzyme is holoenzyme.
  • Cofactors include metal ions and coenzymes. Tightly bound coenzymes are prosthetic groups; cosubstrates bind transiently.
  • The active site binds substrate and performs catalysis. Specificity may be absolute, group, bond or stereospecific.
  • Six classical EC classes are oxidoreductases, transferases, hydrolases, lyases, isomerases and ligases; translocases are now recognized as an additional class.
  • Catalytic strategies include acid-base catalysis, covalent catalysis, metal-ion catalysis, proximity and transition-state stabilization.
  • Lock-and-key explains complementarity; induced fit better describes conformational adaptation after substrate binding.
  • Isoenzymes catalyze the same reaction but differ in structure or tissue distribution, allowing clinical localization.

Flowchart or diagram to draw

E + S <-> ES -> EP -> E + P; enzyme lowers activation energy but not delta G or equilibrium

Clinical relevance

  • Many vitamin deficiencies impair coenzyme-dependent reactions. Organophosphates irreversibly inhibit acetylcholinesterase.
  • Inherited enzyme deficiency causes substrate accumulation, product deficiency and diversion to alternate pathways.

11Enzymes 2

Top
OpeningCorePathwayClinicalFinish
1 mark4 marks2 marks2 marks1 mark

Definition and importance

Enzyme kinetics describes reaction rate as a function of substrate concentration and permits estimation of affinity, capacity and inhibition.

Core answer

  • Michaelis-Menten equation is v = Vmax[S]/(Km + [S]) for many single-substrate enzymes under initial-rate conditions.
  • Vmax is the rate when enzyme is saturated and is proportional to enzyme concentration. Km equals substrate concentration at half Vmax and often inversely reflects apparent affinity.
  • At low substrate, reaction is first order with respect to substrate; near saturation it becomes zero order.
  • Competitive inhibition increases apparent Km with unchanged Vmax and can be overcome by substrate. Noncompetitive inhibition lowers Vmax with unchanged Km in the ideal case.
  • Uncompetitive inhibition lowers both Km and Vmax. Irreversible inhibition permanently inactivates enzyme by covalent or extremely tight binding.
  • Lineweaver-Burk plot is a double-reciprocal linear plot useful for teaching inhibition, though nonlinear regression is preferred experimentally.
  • Turnover number kcat is substrate molecules converted per enzyme per second at saturation; kcat/Km measures catalytic efficiency.

Flowchart or diagram to draw

As [S] rises: v approaches Vmax; at [S] = Km, v = one-half Vmax

Clinical relevance

  • Methanol is treated with fomepizole or ethanol by competition for alcohol dehydrogenase. Statins competitively inhibit HMG-CoA reductase.
  • Aspirin irreversibly acetylates cyclooxygenase; cyanide inhibits cytochrome oxidase and blocks oxidative phosphorylation.

12Enzymes 3

Top
OpeningCorePathwayClinicalFinish
1 mark4 marks2 marks2 marks1 mark

Definition and importance

Enzyme activity is regulated rapidly by allosteric effects and covalent modification, and more slowly by changes in enzyme synthesis or degradation.

Core answer

  • Allosteric enzymes are often multisubunit, show sigmoidal kinetics and bind regulators at sites distinct from the active site.
  • Feedback inhibition occurs when an end product inhibits an early committed step, preventing wasteful synthesis.
  • Reversible phosphorylation by kinases and dephosphorylation by phosphatases coordinate pathways; the effect depends on the enzyme.
  • Zymogens are inactive precursors activated by limited proteolysis, as in digestive enzymes and coagulation factors. Activation is essentially irreversible.
  • Compartmentation separates opposing pathways. Substrate availability, product removal, energy charge and redox state also regulate flux.
  • Induction and repression alter enzyme amount through transcription, translation and degradation. Hormones coordinate regulation across tissues.
  • Enzyme activity is affected by temperature, pH, ionic strength, enzyme concentration, substrate concentration and time.

Flowchart or diagram to draw

Signal -> receptor -> second messenger -> kinase/phosphatase -> enzyme activity -> pathway flux

Clinical relevance

  • Insulin promotes dephosphorylation of several metabolic enzymes, whereas glucagon acts through cAMP-dependent protein kinase in liver.
  • Premature trypsin activation contributes to acute pancreatitis; cascade activation amplifies coagulation but requires strong control.

13Clinically Important Enzymes

Top
OpeningCorePathwayClinicalFinish
1 mark4 marks2 marks2 marks1 mark

Definition and importance

Serum enzymes are measured to detect and monitor cell injury, obstruction or altered synthesis; interpretation depends on tissue distribution, time course and specificity.

Core answer

  • ALT is relatively liver-specific; AST also occurs in heart and skeletal muscle. Marked aminotransferase elevation suggests hepatocellular injury, while the pattern and history refine the cause.
  • Alkaline phosphatase rises in cholestasis and osteoblastic activity. GGT or 5-prime nucleotidase supports a hepatobiliary source of raised ALP.
  • Amylase and especially lipase support acute pancreatitis; lipase is generally more specific and remains elevated longer.
  • Creatine kinase is abundant in muscle; CK-MB may support myocardial injury or reinfarction, but cardiac troponin is the preferred biomarker for acute myocardial infarction.
  • LDH is widespread; isoenzyme patterns are less commonly used now. Hemolysis can falsely elevate LDH, AST and potassium.
  • Acid phosphatase has historical importance in prostate disease; cholinesterase falls in severe liver dysfunction and is inhibited by organophosphates.
  • Sensitivity, specificity, reference limits, biological variation, half-life and serial change determine diagnostic value.

Flowchart or diagram to draw

Tissue injury or obstruction -> enzyme release/retention -> characteristic serum pattern -> serial interpretation

Clinical relevance

  • Hepatocellular pattern: ALT/AST disproportionately high. Cholestatic pattern: ALP/GGT disproportionately high.
  • Enzyme elevation indicates injury or altered clearance, not necessarily loss of organ function; pair it with functional tests.

14Nucleic Acid Chemistry

Top
OpeningCorePathwayClinicalFinish
1 mark4 marks2 marks2 marks1 mark

Definition and importance

Nucleic acids are polymers of nucleotides that store, transmit and express genetic information; nucleotides also participate in energy transfer and signaling.

Core answer

  • A nitrogenous base plus pentose is a nucleoside; addition of phosphate forms a nucleotide. Purines are adenine and guanine; pyrimidines are cytosine, thymine and uracil.
  • DNA contains deoxyribose and A, G, C, T; RNA contains ribose and usually A, G, C, U. Nucleotides are joined by 3-prime,5-prime phosphodiester bonds.
  • DNA double helix is antiparallel and complementary: A pairs with T by two hydrogen bonds, G with C by three. Base stacking adds major stability.
  • B-DNA is the common right-handed form; A-DNA is shorter and wider; Z-DNA is left-handed and may occur transiently in GC-rich sequences.
  • DNA denaturation separates strands; melting temperature rises with GC content and ionic strength. Complementary strands reanneal, enabling hybridization tests.
  • RNA types include mRNA, rRNA, tRNA and regulatory RNAs. RNA is generally single stranded and can form hairpins and catalytic structures.
  • ATP and GTP transfer energy; cAMP and cGMP are second messengers; UDP-sugars and CDP-lipids are activated intermediates.

Flowchart or diagram to draw

Base + sugar = nucleoside; nucleoside + phosphate = nucleotide; nucleotides -> nucleic acid

Clinical relevance

  • Mutations alter sequence, while epigenetic modifications alter expression without changing sequence. DNA/RNA hybridization underlies probes and amplification assays.
  • Purine catabolism produces uric acid; abnormal nucleotide metabolism causes gout, immunodeficiency and orotic aciduria.

Definition and importance

Vitamin A is a fat-soluble vitamin comprising preformed retinoids—retinol, retinal and retinoic acid—and provitamin A carotenoids such as beta-carotene. It is essential for vision, epithelial differentiation, growth, reproduction and immune defence.

1. Forms, sources and daily handling

FormPrincipal biochemical role
RetinolTransport and alcohol form; readily esterified for storage.
RetinalAldehyde used in the visual cycle; interconvertible with retinol.
Retinoic acidHormone-like regulator of gene transcription; cannot return to retinal for vision.
CarotenoidsPlant precursors converted variably to retinal; beta-carotene is the major example.
  • Sources: liver, fish-liver oils, egg yolk, butter and fortified dairy provide preformed vitamin A. Carrots, pumpkin, mango, papaya and dark-green leafy vegetables provide carotenoids.
  • Absorption: dietary retinyl esters are hydrolysed in the intestinal lumen. Retinol and carotenoids enter micelles with the help of bile and pancreatic enzymes, are taken up by enterocytes, re-esterified and exported in chylomicrons through lymph.
  • Storage and transport: chylomicron remnants deliver vitamin A to the liver, where hepatic stellate cells store it mainly as retinyl palmitate. Retinol is released in plasma bound to retinol-binding protein; the complex associates with transthyretin. Zinc is required for normal synthesis and mobilization of retinol-binding protein.

2. Biochemical functions

  • Vision: 11-cis-retinal combines with opsin to form rhodopsin in rod cells. Light isomerizes 11-cis-retinal to all-trans-retinal, changing opsin and activating transducin. Phosphodiesterase lowers cGMP, cGMP-gated sodium channels close and the photoreceptor hyperpolarizes. Retinal is then recycled to restore the visual pigment and permit dark adaptation.
  • Gene regulation: retinoic acid binds nuclear retinoic acid receptors and retinoid X receptors. These receptor complexes bind DNA response elements and regulate proteins needed for epithelial differentiation, growth and development.
  • Epithelial integrity: vitamin A maintains mucus-secreting epithelium and prevents keratinizing metaplasia of conjunctiva, respiratory, gastrointestinal and genitourinary tracts.
  • Growth, reproduction and immunity: it supports skeletal growth, spermatogenesis, embryonic development and barrier and lymphocyte function. Carotenoids also contribute antioxidant activity.

Flowchart or diagram to draw

11-cis-retinal + opsin → rhodopsin —light→ all-trans-retinal → transducin → phosphodiesterase → ↓cGMP → Na+ channel closure → hyperpolarization → visual signal

3. Deficiency and toxicity

  • Causes: poor intake, protein-energy malnutrition, fat malabsorption, cholestasis, pancreatic disease and impaired hepatic storage or transport.
  • Ocular sequence: delayed dark adaptation and night blindness are early features, followed by conjunctival xerosis, Bitot spots, corneal xerosis and keratomalacia. Corneal destruction may cause irreversible blindness.
  • Extraocular features: follicular hyperkeratosis, dry skin, growth retardation, impaired immunity, recurrent infection and reproductive dysfunction.
  • Hypervitaminosis A: acute excess produces nausea, vomiting, headache, blurred vision and raised intracranial pressure. Chronic excess causes dry skin, alopecia, hepatotoxicity, bone pain and fractures. Retinoids are teratogenic, so pregnancy exposure must be avoided. Excess beta-carotene can cause yellow-orange skin discoloration but not classic retinoid toxicity.

Clinical relevance

  • Serum retinol may not fall until hepatic stores are substantially depleted, so clinical context and ocular findings remain important.
  • Retinoid drugs are used in acne and selected malignancies, but require monitoring for liver, lipid and pregnancy-related risks.

Definition and importance

Vitamin D is a fat-soluble prohormone whose active metabolite, calcitriol, acts through a nuclear receptor to maintain calcium–phosphate homeostasis and permit normal mineralization of bone.

1. Sources, absorption and activation

  • Forms and sources: cholecalciferol (D3) is formed in skin and occurs in animal foods; ergocalciferol (D2) is derived from fungi and plants. Useful dietary sources include oily fish, egg yolk, liver and fortified foods. Dietary vitamin D follows the normal fat-absorption pathway in micelles and chylomicrons.
  • Skin: ultraviolet-B radiation converts 7-dehydrocholesterol to previtamin D3, which rearranges to cholecalciferol. It circulates bound to vitamin-D-binding protein.
  • Liver: 25-hydroxylase converts vitamin D to 25-hydroxyvitamin D (calcidiol), the major circulating storage form and usual laboratory indicator of vitamin D status.
  • Kidney: proximal tubular 1-alpha-hydroxylase converts calcidiol to 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (calcitriol), the active hormone. Parathyroid hormone, low calcium and low phosphate favour activation; FGF23, high phosphate and calcitriol feedback oppose it. Renal 24-hydroxylation begins inactivation.

Flowchart or diagram to draw

7-dehydrocholesterol —UVB→ D3 → liver 25-hydroxylase → 25-OH D (calcidiol) → kidney 1α-hydroxylase → 1,25-(OH)2 D (calcitriol) → intestine + kidney + bone

2. Mechanism and physiological actions

Calcitriol binds the intracellular vitamin D receptor. The receptor pairs with RXR and binds vitamin-D response elements, altering gene transcription.

  • Intestine: increases synthesis of calcium transport proteins, including calbindin, and promotes absorption of both calcium and phosphate.
  • Kidney: supports calcium reabsorption and participates with PTH in maintaining extracellular calcium.
  • Bone: supplies calcium and phosphate for mineralization. When calcium is threatened, calcitriol and PTH also coordinate osteoblast signalling and bone resorption to preserve plasma calcium.
  • Parathyroid gland: suppresses excess PTH synthesis, completing a feedback loop. These endocrine actions explain why vitamin D behaves as a hormone rather than merely a dietary factor.

Definition and importance

Vitamin E is a family of fat-soluble tocopherols and tocotrienols. Alpha-tocopherol is preferentially retained in humans and is the major chain-breaking antioxidant of cell membranes and circulating lipoproteins.

1. Forms, sources, absorption and transport

  • Natural vitamin E occurs as alpha, beta, gamma and delta tocopherols and corresponding tocotrienols. Alpha-tocopherol has the greatest nutritional importance because hepatic alpha-tocopherol transfer protein preferentially incorporates it into circulating lipoproteins.
  • Sources: wheat-germ, sunflower, safflower and other vegetable oils; almonds and other nuts; seeds; whole grains and green vegetables.
  • Absorption: it requires dietary fat, pancreatic enzymes and bile salts. Vitamin E enters micelles, is absorbed by enterocytes and leaves in chylomicrons. Chylomicron remnants deliver it to the liver, after which VLDL and other lipoproteins distribute it to tissues. Adipose tissue is a major store.

2. Antioxidant mechanism and functions

Polyunsaturated fatty acids in membranes are susceptible to free-radical attack. Once a lipid radical forms, it reacts with oxygen to create a lipid peroxyl radical that propagates a damaging chain reaction.

  • Alpha-tocopherol donates hydrogen to the lipid peroxyl radical, producing a stable lipid hydroperoxide and a relatively unreactive tocopheroxyl radical. It therefore breaks the propagation chain.
  • Vitamin C and other reducing systems can regenerate active tocopherol. Selenium-dependent glutathione peroxidase removes lipid hydroperoxides, so these antioxidant systems cooperate.
  • Vitamin E preserves erythrocyte membrane integrity and protects neuronal and muscle membranes, lipoproteins and vitamin A from oxidative injury. It also influences cell signalling, platelet aggregation and immune responses.

Flowchart or diagram to draw

Lipid radical + O2 → lipid peroxyl radical —vitamin E-H→ lipid hydroperoxide + tocopheroxyl radical —vitamin C/reducing systems→ regenerated vitamin E

3. Deficiency, assessment and toxicity

  • Deficiency is uncommon with an ordinary diet. It occurs mainly in severe or prolonged fat malabsorption, cholestatic disease, abetalipoproteinemia and some very-low-birth-weight premature infants.
  • Hematologic: increased oxidative fragility of erythrocytes produces hemolytic anemia, especially in premature infants.
  • Neuromuscular: peripheral neuropathy, hyporeflexia, spinocerebellar ataxia, loss of vibration and position sense, skeletal myopathy and, in severe disease, retinopathy.
  • Assessment: plasma alpha-tocopherol can be interpreted in relation to total circulating lipids because vitamin E travels in lipoproteins.
  • Excess: food sources are not a usual cause of toxicity, but high-dose supplements can impair normal coagulation and increase bleeding risk, particularly with anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy.

Clinical relevance

  • Abetalipoproteinemia prevents normal chylomicron and lipoprotein transport, making early vitamin E deficiency a characteristic problem.
  • Treatment must correct the underlying malabsorption where possible; routine high-dose antioxidant supplementation is not a substitute for a balanced diet or disease-specific care.

Definition and importance

Vitamin K is a fat-soluble vitamin that serves as the cofactor for gamma-carboxylation of selected glutamate residues, enabling coagulation and bone proteins to bind calcium and function normally.

1. Forms, sources and absorption

  • Vitamin K1 (phylloquinone): the chief dietary form, abundant in green leafy vegetables such as spinach, cabbage and broccoli.
  • Vitamin K2 (menaquinones): present in some animal and fermented foods and synthesized by intestinal bacteria. The exact nutritional contribution of colonic synthesis is limited by its site of absorption.
  • Vitamin K3 (menadione): a synthetic compound of historical importance; unlike natural K1/K2, it can produce toxicity in infants.
  • As a fat-soluble vitamin, K is absorbed with micelles in the presence of bile and pancreatic function, enters chylomicrons and is delivered to the liver. Body stores are comparatively small, so interruption of intake or absorption may become clinically evident.

2. Gamma-carboxylation and the vitamin K cycle

Reduced vitamin K hydroquinone is a cofactor for hepatic gamma-glutamyl carboxylase. The enzyme converts selected glutamate residues to gamma-carboxyglutamate (Gla) residues while vitamin K is oxidized to its epoxide.

  • Gla residues bind calcium, allowing the protein to attach to negatively charged phospholipid surfaces and participate effectively in coagulation.
  • Vitamin K–dependent proteins are remembered as 1972 + C and S: factors II, VII, IX and X, plus the natural anticoagulant proteins C and S. Osteocalcin and matrix Gla protein are additional vitamin K–dependent proteins in bone and soft tissues.
  • Vitamin K epoxide reductase (VKOR) recycles the epoxide toward the active reduced form. Warfarin inhibits VKOR, so newly synthesized proteins remain undercarboxylated and biologically weak.

Definition and importance

Definition and importance

Beta oxidation is the mitochondrial spiral that converts fatty acyl-CoA to acetyl-CoA, NADH and FADH2 for energy production.

  • Fasting hormones promote adipose lipolysis; liver oxidizes fatty acids and may convert acetyl-CoA to ketone bodies.

  • HDL begins with apoA-I, collects cholesterol through ABCA1, and LCAT esterifies it. HDL returns cholesterol directly to liver or exchanges esters through CETP.
  • ApoA-I activates LCAT; apoC-II activates LPL; apoE supports remnant uptake; apoB-48 and apoB-100 are structural proteins.
  • Oxidized or modified LDL is taken up by macrophage scavenger receptors, forming foam cells and contributing to atherosclerosis.
  • Familial hypercholesterolemia results from impaired LDL clearance through defects in LDLR, apoB or PCSK9-related pathways.

Flowchart or diagram to draw

Exogenous: gut -> chylomicron -> remnant -> liver; endogenous: liver -> VLDL -> IDL -> LDL; HDL reverse transport

Clinical relevance

  • Very high triglycerides increase pancreatitis risk; LDL-containing apoB particles drive atherosclerotic risk.
  • Fasting is not always required for a standard lipid profile, but may help when triglycerides are very high or required by local protocol.

71Cholesterol Metabolism

Top

1 mark4 marks2 marks2 marks1 mark

Definition and importance

Cholesterol is synthesized from acetyl-CoA, absorbed from intestine, transported in lipoproteins and eliminated mainly through bile acids and biliary cholesterol.

Core answer

  • Cytosolic acetyl-CoA forms HMG-CoA; HMG-CoA reductase converts it to mevalonate and is the rate-limiting step.
  • Mevalonate forms activated isoprenes, squalene, lanosterol and cholesterol. The pathway also makes nonsterol isoprenoids.
  • HMG-CoA reductase is activated by insulin and dephosphorylation and inhibited by cholesterol feedback, AMPK-mediated phosphorylation and statins.
  • SREBP increases transcription when cellular cholesterol is low. LDL-receptor uptake supplies cholesterol; ACAT esterifies intracellular cholesterol for storage.
  • CYP7A1 catalyzes the rate-limiting step in bile-acid synthesis. Bile acids aid lipid digestion and undergo enterohepatic circulation.
  • Humans cannot degrade the sterol nucleus completely; elimination is through fecal bile acids and neutral sterols.
  • Cholesterol is the precursor of steroid hormones, vitamin D and bile acids and is an essential membrane component.

Acetyl-CoA -> HMG-CoA --HMG-CoA reductase--> mevalonate -> isoprenes -> squalene -> cholesterol -> bile acids/steroids

  • Statins lower hepatic cholesterol, upregulate LDL receptors and reduce circulating LDL. Bile-acid sequestrants interrupt enterohepatic circulation.
  • Cholesterol supersaturation and impaired gallbladder function contribute to cholesterol gallstones.

72Lipid Profile and Cardiac Markers

Top

1 mark4 marks2 marks2 marks1 mark

Definition and importance

A lipid profile estimates atherogenic lipid burden, while cardiac biomarkers detect myocardial injury and help diagnose acute coronary syndromes.

Core answer

  • Standard lipid measures are total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, triglycerides and calculated or directly measured LDL cholesterol. Non-HDL cholesterol equals total minus HDL.
  • ApoB estimates the number of atherogenic particles; lipoprotein(a) is a largely inherited risk enhancer measured selectively or at least once according to many guidelines.
  • LDL may be calculated when triglycerides are not too high; equation choice and local laboratory limits matter. Nonfasting samples are often adequate.
  • Cardiac troponin I or T is the preferred marker of myocardial injury. Acute myocardial infarction requires a rise/fall with at least one value above the assay's 99th percentile plus evidence of ischemia.
  • High-sensitivity troponin algorithms use serial change and timing. Elevated troponin is not synonymous with infarction and also occurs in myocarditis, heart failure, renal disease and critical illness.
  • CK-MB may help in selected settings such as suspected reinfarction but is less specific than troponin. Myoglobin rises early but is nonspecific.
  • BNP or NT-proBNP supports diagnosis and prognosis in heart failure but varies with age, renal function, rhythm and obesity.

Atherosclerotic risk: LDL/non-HDL/apoB; chest pain: ECG + serial troponin + clinical evidence -> myocardial injury versus infarction

Clinical relevance

  • Risk treatment decisions combine lipids with overall cardiovascular risk, comorbidities and prior disease, not a single number.
  • Pre-analytical timing and assay-specific reference limits are essential when interpreting troponin.

73Nucleic Acid Metabolism 1 Purines

Top
OpeningCorePathwayClinicalFinish
1 mark4 marks2 marks2 marks1 mark

Definition and importance

Purine nucleotides are synthesized de novo on a ribose-phosphate scaffold, salvaged from bases and degraded to uric acid in humans.

Core answer

  • Ribose-5-phosphate forms PRPP. Glutamine-PRPP amidotransferase is the committed step; atoms are added to the ribose to form IMP.
  • IMP branches to AMP and GMP. AMP synthesis uses GTP; GMP synthesis uses ATP, balancing nucleotide pools.
  • Feedback inhibition by AMP, GMP and IMP controls de novo synthesis. Ribonucleotide reductase forms deoxyribonucleotides and is tightly regulated.
  • HGPRT salvages hypoxanthine and guanine; APRT salvages adenine. Salvage conserves energy and reduces uric-acid production.
  • AMP and GMP degrade through hypoxanthine or xanthine to uric acid by xanthine oxidase.
  • Hyperuricemia may cause gout and stones. Allopurinol and febuxostat inhibit xanthine oxidase; uricosuric strategies increase renal urate loss.
  • HGPRT deficiency causes Lesch-Nyhan syndrome; adenosine deaminase deficiency causes severe combined immunodeficiency through toxic purine metabolites.

Flowchart or diagram to draw

Ribose-5-P -> PRPP -> IMP -> AMP/GMP -> hypoxanthine/xanthine -> uric acid; bases -> salvage

Clinical relevance

  • Tumor lysis syndrome releases nucleic acids and may cause hyperuricemia, hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, hypocalcemia and acute kidney injury.
  • Humans lack uricase, so uric acid is the final purine product; recombinant uricase can be used in selected high-risk settings.

Definition and importance

Pyrimidine nucleotides are synthesized by building the ring first and then attaching it to PRPP; degradation yields soluble products.

Core answer

  • UMP synthase deficiency causes hereditary orotic aciduria with megaloblastic anemia and growth failure; uridine bypasses the defect.

75Immunoglobulins

Top
OpeningCorePathwayClinicalFinish
1 mark4 marks2 marks2 marks1 mark

Definition and importance

Immunoglobulins are antigen-binding glycoproteins produced by B cells and plasma cells that mediate humoral immunity.

Core answer

  • A basic antibody has two identical heavy and two identical light chains linked by disulfide bonds. Variable regions bind antigen; constant regions determine class and effector function.
  • Papain produces two Fab fragments and one Fc fragment. The hinge provides flexibility; Fc binds complement receptors and Fc receptors.
  • IgG is the major serum antibody, crosses placenta, opsonizes and activates complement. IgA protects mucosa and secretions, usually as a dimer with J chain and secretory component.
  • IgM is a pentamer in plasma, appears first in primary response and strongly activates complement. IgE mediates immediate hypersensitivity and helminth defense. IgD functions mainly as a B-cell receptor.
  • Diversity arises from V(D)J recombination, junctional diversity and chain combination; activation adds somatic hypermutation and class-switch recombination.
  • Primary response has a lag and early IgM; secondary response is faster, stronger and higher affinity because of memory cells.
  • Complement activation, neutralization, opsonization and antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity are major effector mechanisms.

Flowchart or diagram to draw

2 heavy + 2 light chains -> Fab antigen binding + Fc effector function; class switching changes Fc, not specificity

Clinical relevance

  • Monoclonal gammopathy produces a restricted immunoglobulin; immunodeficiency may reduce one or more classes.
  • Maternal IgG provides fetal/neonatal systemic protection, while breast-milk secretory IgA protects mucosal surfaces.

76Inborn Errors of Metabolism

Top
OpeningCorePathwayClinicalFinish
1 mark4 marks2 marks2 marks1 mark

Definition and importance

Inborn errors of metabolism are inherited defects of enzymes, transporters or cofactors that cause substrate accumulation, product deficiency or toxic alternate metabolites.

Core answer

  • Most are autosomal recessive, though important X-linked and mitochondrial disorders exist. Presentation may be neonatal, episodic after fasting/illness or chronic and progressive.
  • Pathophysiologic groups include intoxication disorders, energy-deficiency disorders and disorders of complex molecules or organelles.
  • Red flags are poor feeding, vomiting, lethargy, seizures, unexplained acidosis, hyperammonemia, hypoglycemia, unusual odor, hepatomegaly or regression.
  • Initial critical samples during illness include glucose, blood gas, electrolytes/anion gap, lactate, ammonia, ketones, liver tests and urine; specialized tests include amino acids, acylcarnitines and urine organic acids.
  • Examples include phenylketonuria, maple-syrup urine disease, homocystinuria, urea-cycle defects, organic acidemias, fatty-acid oxidation disorders and lysosomal storage diseases.
  • Treatment principles are stop catabolism, provide safe calories, remove toxic metabolites, restrict the offending substrate, replace products/cofactors and use dialysis or scavengers when indicated.
  • Newborn screening detects selected disorders before symptoms; confirmatory testing and lifelong specialist follow-up remain essential.

Flowchart or diagram to draw

Gene defect -> enzyme/transporter defect -> substrate/toxin rises + product falls -> acute or chronic phenotype -> targeted therapy

Clinical relevance

  • A normal newborn screen does not exclude every metabolic disease; acute unexplained encephalopathy still requires urgent evaluation.
  • Avoid prolonged fasting in suspected fatty-acid oxidation disorders, which characteristically cause hypoketotic hypoglycemia.

77DNA Replication

Top
OpeningCorePathwayClinicalFinish
1 mark4 marks2 marks2 marks1 mark

Definition and importance

DNA replication is semiconservative, bidirectional synthesis of complementary DNA before cell division, with high fidelity and defined origins.

Core answer

  • Helicase unwinds DNA; single-strand binding proteins stabilize strands; topoisomerases relieve supercoiling. Primase makes RNA primers.
  • DNA polymerases synthesize only 5-prime to 3-prime while reading template 3-prime to 5-prime and require a primer with free 3-prime OH.
  • Leading strand is synthesized continuously; lagging strand forms Okazaki fragments. Primer removal, gap filling and ligase action create a continuous strand.
  • Eukaryotic polymerase alpha-primase initiates, polymerase epsilon mainly extends leading strand and polymerase delta mainly extends lagging strand; PCNA acts as sliding clamp.
  • Proofreading 3-prime to 5-prime exonuclease and postreplication mismatch repair produce high fidelity.
  • Linear chromosome ends require telomerase, an RNA-dependent DNA polymerase active in germ cells, stem cells and many cancers but limited in most somatic cells.
  • Replication occurs in S phase and is licensed so each origin fires once per cycle.

Flowchart or diagram to draw

Origin -> helicase fork -> leading continuous + lagging Okazaki fragments -> primer removal -> ligase -> proofreading/repair

Clinical relevance

  • Defective mismatch repair causes microsatellite instability and predisposes to Lynch syndrome.
  • Several drugs target DNA replication or nucleotide synthesis; rapidly dividing normal tissues therefore also experience toxicity.

78Genetic Code and Mutation

Top
OpeningCorePathwayClinicalFinish
1 mark4 marks2 marks2 marks1 mark

Definition and importance

The genetic code maps mRNA nucleotide triplets to amino acids; mutations are stable sequence changes that may alter RNA, protein or regulation.

Core answer

  • The code is triplet, nonoverlapping, comma-free, nearly universal and degenerate but unambiguous. AUG usually initiates; UAA, UAG and UGA terminate.
  • Degeneracy often lies at the third wobble position, allowing one tRNA to recognize multiple codons.
  • Point substitutions are transitions or transversions and may be synonymous, missense or nonsense.
  • Insertions or deletions not divisible by three cause frameshift; splice-site, promoter, enhancer and repeat-expansion mutations affect expression or processing.
  • Loss-of-function, gain-of-function, dominant-negative and haploinsufficiency describe functional consequences. Germline variants are heritable; somatic variants are confined to a clone or tissue.
  • Mutagens include radiation, alkylating agents, base analogs and intercalators. Spontaneous depurination, deamination and replication error also occur.
  • DNA repair systems include direct reversal, base-excision, nucleotide-excision, mismatch and double-strand-break repair.

Flowchart or diagram to draw

DNA change -> RNA effect -> codon/splicing/expression change -> protein effect -> phenotype

Clinical relevance

  • Sickle cell disease is a missense example; beta-thalassemia may result from nonsense or splice variants; Duchenne muscular dystrophy often involves frameshift/deletion effects.
  • Variant pathogenicity depends on population frequency, segregation, functional evidence and phenotype; not every detected variant causes disease.

79Transcription

Top
OpeningCorePathwayClinicalFinish
1 mark4 marks2 marks2 marks1 mark

Definition and importance

Transcription is synthesis of RNA from a DNA template by RNA polymerase, followed in eukaryotes by extensive RNA processing.

Core answer

  • RNA polymerase reads DNA 3-prime to 5-prime and synthesizes RNA 5-prime to 3-prime without a primer.
  • In eukaryotes RNA polymerase I makes most rRNA, polymerase II makes mRNA and several regulatory RNAs, and polymerase III makes tRNA and 5S rRNA.
  • General transcription factors assemble at promoters such as the TATA-containing core; enhancers, silencers and chromatin state regulate initiation.
  • Transcription proceeds through initiation, elongation and termination. The RNA sequence matches coding strand except U replaces T.
  • Pre-mRNA receives a 5-prime cap, introns are removed by spliceosome, and the 3-prime end is cleaved and polyadenylated.
  • Alternative splicing and RNA editing increase proteomic diversity; mRNA stability, localization and microRNAs regulate expression after transcription.
  • Bacterial transcription differs by a single core RNA polymerase with sigma factors and can be coupled to translation.

Flowchart or diagram to draw

DNA promoter -> initiation -> RNA elongation -> termination -> cap + splicing + poly(A) -> mature mRNA

Clinical relevance

  • Alpha-amanitin strongly inhibits RNA polymerase II; rifampicin inhibits bacterial RNA polymerase.
  • Splice-site mutations can cause beta-thalassemia and other genetic diseases even when the coding amino-acid sequence is not directly altered.

80Translation

Top
OpeningCorePathwayClinicalFinish
1 mark4 marks2 marks2 marks1 mark

Definition and importance

Translation decodes mRNA on ribosomes to synthesize a polypeptide in the 5-prime-to-3-prime mRNA direction and N-to-C protein direction.

Core answer

  • Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases charge tRNAs using ATP and provide the first level of decoding accuracy. The anticodon recognizes the mRNA codon.
  • Ribosomes have A, P and E sites. Eukaryotic 80S ribosomes contain 40S and 60S subunits; bacterial 70S ribosomes contain 30S and 50S.
  • Initiation identifies AUG and places initiator methionyl-tRNA in P site. Eukaryotes usually scan from the 5-prime cap through a Kozak context.
  • During elongation, aminoacyl-tRNA enters A site, rRNA peptidyl transferase forms the peptide bond and translocation moves the ribosome.
  • Stop codons recruit release factors, hydrolyzing the completed chain. ATP and GTP are consumed in charging, initiation, delivery, translocation and termination.
  • Signal peptides and signal-recognition particle target secreted and membrane proteins to rough ER; other targeting sequences direct proteins to organelles.
  • Post-translational modifications include cleavage, phosphorylation, glycosylation, hydroxylation, acetylation, lipidation and ubiquitination.

Flowchart or diagram to draw

mRNA + charged tRNAs + ribosome -> initiation -> A/P/E elongation cycle -> stop/release -> folding and modification

Clinical relevance

  • Many antibiotics selectively target bacterial translation: aminoglycosides affect decoding, tetracyclines block A-site entry and macrolides inhibit translocation.
  • Diphtheria toxin inactivates eukaryotic elongation factor 2; ricin damages rRNA.

81Genetic Technologies

Top
OpeningCorePathwayClinicalFinish
1 mark4 marks2 marks2 marks1 mark

Definition and importance

Genetic technologies detect, amplify, sequence, modify and interpret nucleic acids for diagnosis, research, forensics and therapy.

Core answer

  • PCR requires template DNA, two primers, thermostable polymerase, dNTPs and cycles of denaturation, annealing and extension. RT-PCR first converts RNA to cDNA; real-time PCR quantifies amplification using fluorescence.
  • Gel electrophoresis separates nucleic acids by size. Restriction enzymes cut specific sequences; ligase joins DNA; plasmid vectors enable cloning and expression.
  • Sanger sequencing uses chain-terminating nucleotides and is useful for targeted regions. Next-generation sequencing analyzes many fragments in parallel for panels, exomes or genomes.
  • Hybridization methods include Southern blot for DNA, Northern blot for RNA and probe-based arrays or in situ techniques.
  • CRISPR-Cas systems use guide RNA to target nucleic acid; editing can disrupt genes or introduce changes, but off-target effects and delivery are major challenges.
  • Diagnostic workflow includes appropriate consent, specimen quality, variant calling, confirmation when needed, interpretation and genetic counseling.
  • Applications include prenatal diagnosis, pathogen detection, cancer profiling, pharmacogenomics, identity testing and gene therapy.

Flowchart or diagram to draw

Sample -> extraction -> amplification/hybridization/sequencing -> quality control -> interpretation -> counseling or treatment

Clinical relevance

  • Analytical sensitivity is not clinical significance: a detected variant may be benign, uncertain or pathogenic depending on evidence.
  • Ethical issues include privacy, incidental findings, discrimination, germline implications, equitable access and limits of embryo/germline editing.
